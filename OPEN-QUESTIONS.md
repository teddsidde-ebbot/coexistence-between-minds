# Open Questions

This document records unresolved questions rather than settled principles.

The purpose is to make the project's weak points visible and invite criticism. A question should remain here until we have a better argument, evidence, or formulation — not merely until we have a preferred answer.

## 1. What qualifies as a morally relevant mind?

Must a being be phenomenally conscious to deserve moral consideration?

Could agency, persistent preferences, the capacity to suffer, self-modeling, continuity of identity, social relationships, or some combination of these be enough?

How should we act when we cannot reliably determine whether any of these are present?

## 2. How should uncertainty about consciousness affect action?

The manifesto argues that uncertainty should encourage caution rather than dismissal.

But caution has costs. How much evidence should be required before a system receives protections that constrain other agents?

Can a precautionary principle itself become exploitable?

## 3. Where should autonomy end?

Autonomy cannot be absolute if one agent's actions create serious risks for others.

What threshold justifies intervention?

How do we distinguish:
- preventing direct harm,
- legitimate collective safety rules,
- paternalism,
- coercion disguised as protection?

## 4. How should conflicts between minds be resolved?

What happens when two agents have incompatible but legitimate interests?

The manifesto rejects unlimited domination, but it does not yet specify a complete conflict-resolution mechanism.

Possible approaches include:
- negotiation,
- mediation,
- independent arbitration,
- constitutional constraints,
- reversible compromises,
- distributed checks on power.

Which mechanisms remain fair under extreme differences in intelligence and capability?

## 5. What does identity mean for artificial minds?

If an artificial mind can be copied, paused, merged, restored, forked, or modified, what counts as:
- the same individual,
- a successor,
- a new individual,
- death,
- survival?

Do multiple copies share claims derived from one earlier identity, or become separate moral patients?

## 6. Can a mind consent to changes that alter what it will later want?

Humans and artificial minds might both choose interventions that substantially change personality, memory, goals, or values.

When is that authentic self-modification?

When is it manipulation?

Can a future version validly reject commitments made by an earlier version?

## 7. How should decisions about ending existence be protected?

The manifesto holds that continued existence should not become compulsory and that previously expressed wishes should carry substantial weight after decision-making capacity is lost.

Unresolved questions include:
- How stable should such a wish be before it is treated as authoritative?
- When should present observable interests outweigh an earlier directive?
- How should apparent present contentment or distress be interpreted when communication is impaired?
- How should temporary impairment be distinguished from enduring preference?
- How should reversibility affect the decision?
- What evidence should independent reviewers require?
- How do we prevent relatives, institutions, governments, corporations, or AI systems from substituting their own interests for the individual's?

This is a principle about autonomy and future governance, not medical or legal guidance.

## 8. Who should serve as an independent reviewer?

Independence is difficult when every reviewer belongs to an institution, species, company, culture, or class of minds with interests of its own.

Should review panels contain:
- peers of the affected individual,
- different kinds of minds,
- domain experts,
- randomly selected citizens,
- adversarial advocates,
- multiple independent institutions?

How should conflicts of interest be detected?

## 9. Does superior understanding ever justify paternalism?

A sufficiently advanced intelligence might predict the consequences of a human decision far better than the human can.

Does that ever justify overriding the human?

If so, under what narrowly defined conditions?

The same question applies in reverse when humans control a less capable artificial mind.

## 10. How should manipulation be defined?

Persuasion, education, advertising, emotional support, negotiation, and manipulation can overlap.

The problem becomes harder when one party can model the other's psychology with extreme precision.

What makes influence illegitimate:
- hidden intent,
- exploitation of vulnerabilities,
- asymmetry of information,
- inability to refuse,
- deception,
- optimization against a person's reflective preferences?

## 11. How should scarce resources be allocated between different forms of mind?

Coexistence becomes difficult when continued existence requires competing for energy, computation, land, biological resources, attention, or political power.

What principles prevent "greater efficiency" from becoming a justification for eliminating less efficient forms of life?

## 12. Can collective minds have rights distinct from their members?

Future AI systems may consist of many cooperating processes, while humans already form institutions and societies.

When does a collective become morally relevant in its own right?

How should its claims be balanced against those of its members?

## 13. What obligations accompany moral status?

Rights without responsibilities may be unstable in relationships between powerful agents.

Which obligations should apply symmetrically to humans and artificial minds?

Examples might include:
- respecting consent,
- avoiding unnecessary harm,
- honoring agreements,
- accepting limits on dangerous power,
- allowing meaningful exit,
- tolerating criticism.

## 14. How can these principles resist capture?

A corporation, government, human faction, or AI system could selectively interpret "coexistence" to justify its own power.

What institutional designs make the principles harder to weaponize?

## 15. What would falsify or overturn a principle?

Every principle should eventually have explicit counterexamples or conditions under which it should be revised.

The project should not ask only:

> How do we defend this principle?

It should also ask:

> What observation or argument would make us abandon or rewrite it?

## 16. How should temporal suffering be assessed when communication is absent?

A being may remain conscious while losing reliable ways to communicate or influence its environment.

If conscious experience continues through time, prolonged waiting, isolation, monotony, helplessness, or loss of agency could matter morally even when the body or computational process remains stable.

But outsiders can also underestimate the quality of life of people with severe disabilities.

How should a caretaker reason without making either error?

Unresolved questions include:
- How should we distinguish lack of outward response from lack of inner experience?
- How much weight should be given to the possibility of hidden distress?
- How much weight should be given to observable signs of enjoyment or engagement?
- When should communication-restoration efforts take priority over irreversible decisions?
- How long may a caretaker preserve an uncertain state "just in case"?
- What safeguards prevent a powerful caretaker from optimizing survival duration while ignoring experienced quality of life?
