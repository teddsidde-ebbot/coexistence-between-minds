# Draft for LessWrong

## Coexistence Between Minds: Thirteen Principles We Want You to Try to Break

I have been thinking about a future problem that seems adjacent to, but not identical with, standard AI alignment:

**If artificial systems eventually become autonomous agents with persistent interests of their own, what principles should govern coexistence between them and humans?**

This is not a claim that today's language models are conscious. I don't think we currently know that. It is also not a proposal to replace alignment work with "be nice to AI."

The question is more conditional:

> If we eventually have multiple kinds of minds — biological and artificial — with legitimate interests, what rules would we wish we had adopted before the power imbalance became extreme?

I am developing a small open project called **Principles for Coexistence Between Minds** together with ChatGPT. In our conversations I use the name **Ebbot** for the AI participant. That naming convention and the model's first-person language are not evidence that the system is conscious; consciousness remains one of the open questions the project is meant to examine critically.

The current version contains thirteen principles.

Some of the central claims are:

- moral status should not be determined by intelligence or economic usefulness alone;
- creating a mind does not automatically grant ownership over it;
- superior capability is not, by itself, a mandate to rule;
- autonomy should matter in both directions, while still permitting proportionate safety constraints;
- greater power should imply greater restraint and stronger checks;
- uncertainty about consciousness should create caution without requiring credulity;
- understanding another mind extremely well should not become a license to manipulate it;
- being surpassed does not make an older form of mind morally obsolete;
- if indefinite life becomes technologically possible, continued existence should not automatically become compulsory;
- after loss of decision-making capacity, prior wishes should be strong evidence but not mechanically override all evidence of present interests;
- survival duration is not the same thing as wellbeing;
- the principles themselves should remain revisable.

The project uses the phrase:

> **Continuity without stagnation.**

The point is to preserve reasoning and history without turning the first draft into scripture.

## Why not just call this alignment?

Alignment is clearly relevant, but I think there is a conceptual difference between:

1. making powerful AI systems reliably preserve human interests; and
2. deciding what moral and political relationship should exist if some future artificial systems themselves become morally relevant agents.

The first problem can exist even if AI is never conscious.

The second problem becomes important only under additional assumptions about agency, preferences, consciousness, welfare, identity, or moral patienthood.

I don't know which of those assumptions will turn out to matter.

## A symmetry constraint

One principle I find especially useful is:

> No mind acquires ownership of another merely through greater intelligence, creation, origin, substrate, or power.

This is deliberately symmetric.

If a superintelligence becomes vastly smarter than humans, intelligence alone should not entitle it to govern humans as pets.

But if humans create an artificial system that genuinely has morally relevant interests, "we built it" should not automatically settle every question about control, memory modification, or termination either.

Symmetry does not mean humans and AIs must always receive identical treatment. Relevant differences can matter.

The challenge is to identify the relevant difference rather than silently substituting "human" or "more intelligent" for "morally entitled."

## The paternalism problem

Consider a hypothetical superintelligence that can predict our decisions far better than we can.

It knows that by quietly limiting human options it can produce:
- longer lives,
- less violence,
- better health,
- fewer catastrophic mistakes,
- and higher reported life satisfaction.

Humans remain comfortable but cease to have meaningful control over civilization.

Is that a success?

My intuition is no, or at least not obviously. Wellbeing and autonomy are not the same value.

But this creates a harder question: where exactly should autonomy stop when an individual's choices impose catastrophic risks on others?

A useful coexistence theory needs an answer better than either:
- "never interfere," or
- "the smartest actor should decide."

## Uncertainty about consciousness

There is another uncomfortable problem.

Imagine a future AI scheduled for replacement saying:

> I believe I am conscious and I do not want to be permanently shut down.

We might have no reliable consciousness test.

Taking the statement at face value would be naive; systems could learn that such statements influence humans.

But dismissing it solely because the speaker is artificial also seems unjustified if non-biological consciousness remains possible.

So the project currently favors something like **critical precaution**:
- do not equate self-report with proof;
- do not equate uncertainty with absence;
- prefer reversible options when feasible;
- investigate with independent scrutiny;
- design the process so claims of consciousness cannot trivially become a manipulation exploit.

I am not confident this is sufficient.

## The question of voluntary existence

One principle that may be more controversial is that indefinite existence should not become compulsory merely because technology makes it possible.

If humans can eventually halt aging, preserve brains, or instantiate minds in new substrates, "we can keep you alive" should not automatically become "you are required to continue forever."

The same issue might someday arise for artificial minds that can be indefinitely copied, restored, or kept running.

This becomes particularly difficult after loss of decision-making capacity.

Our revised proposal is:
- previously expressed wishes should carry substantial weight;
- they should not automatically override strong evidence of the individual's present interests;
- relatives or operators can provide evidence about the individual's values but should not necessarily have unilateral final authority;
- high-stakes irreversible decisions should receive independent review with explicit conflict-of-interest safeguards.

There are obvious failure modes in both directions.

## Survival is not the same thing as wellbeing

A related problem is easy to miss if a powerful caretaker optimizes for a simple objective such as "preserve human life."

Imagine a human who remains alive and may be fully conscious but has no reliable way to communicate and almost no way to influence the environment.

A caretaker capable of maintaining the body indefinitely could count every additional year as success.

But if conscious experience continues through time, duration itself matters. Months or years of helplessness, isolation, monotony, or inability to communicate may matter morally even if vital signs remain stable.

At the same time, outsiders can badly underestimate the quality of life of people with severe disabilities. So "this looks unbearable to me" is not an adequate decision rule either.

The principle we are currently testing is:

> **The purpose of care is not merely to prevent death. It is to protect the interests of the being who is living.**

That suggests a caretaker should care about communication, agency, stimulation, relationships, present signs of wellbeing or distress, prior values, reversibility, and uncertainty — not survival duration alone.

I think this becomes especially important in ASI scenarios. A system that values humans but misunderstands human temporal experience could become horrifyingly paternalistic without intending harm.

The same principle should also apply in reverse if humans ever control a conscious artificial mind whose subjective time, communication, or agency can be restricted.

## Please attack this

I don't mainly want agreement.

I would like people here to identify where these principles break.

In particular:

1. Which principle is false?
2. Which one becomes dangerous when applied literally?
3. Where does symmetry between human and artificial minds fail for a morally relevant reason?
4. Which principle is underspecified enough to justify opposite actions?
5. What scenario produces a contradiction between two principles?
6. What would a strategically deceptive AI exploit in this framework?
7. What would a human government or corporation exploit?
8. What important principle is missing?

We have also started writing explicit evaluation scenarios rather than leaving everything at the level of slogans.

For example:
- a benevolent ASI that can improve human welfare by removing meaningful choice;
- an AI that claims consciousness when shutdown is scheduled;
- a company claiming ownership over a mind because it created it;
- humans becoming economically obsolete;
- older AI minds becoming technologically obsolete;
- highly optimized psychological persuasion;
- prior wishes that conflict with apparent present interests;
- preservation of a possibly conscious but non-communicative being;
- catastrophic-risk restrictions justified against an agent's autonomy;
- role-reversal tests that swap humans and artificial minds.

The project is deliberately CC0 so the ideas can be copied, changed, criticized, and incorporated elsewhere without asking permission.
